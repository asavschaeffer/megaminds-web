## why-it-matters

Every other model in this section wrote its own report. Fable 5's is editorial instead — but not because it was always locked away. For three days in June 2026 it was the opposite of locked away: included at no extra cost on Pro, Max, and Team plans, callable by anyone with a Claude subscription, the first time Anthropic's top-tier Mythos capability was handed to the general public. Then it became something rarer still — the first deployed, publicly available AI model recalled by a US government export-control order. <Sidenote label="we used it, and it was glorious">**Editorial note — Megaminds.** This isn't secondhand. We called Fable 5 during its brief public window, and it was, in a word, glorious — the reason this report exists at all is that the suspension took away something we'd actually gotten to use. We're keeping the hard specs as `TODO(research)` rather than reconstructing them from memory, but the access story below is firsthand.</Sidenote> Asked to write its own report now, it returns a single line: *"Claude Fable 5 is currently unavailable."* It matters not for a benchmark it tops but for the arc it traces — a frontier capability opened to everyone, then pulled by national-security authority within the week — and for what that says about who ultimately controls access to the frontier.

## the-opening

On June 9, 2026, Anthropic released Fable 5 as, in its own words, "the first publicly available version of its Mythos model." It went live immediately on the Claude API and consumption-based Enterprise plans, and the announcement promised it free on Pro, Max, Team, and seat-based Enterprise plans "from today through June 22," after which access would move to usage credits. <Sidenote label="full safeguards, with a fallback">Fable shipped with three classifier systems watching for cybersecurity, biology/chemistry, and distillation misuse. When one triggers, the request is "automatically handled by Claude Opus 4.8 instead" — so the guardrails degrade gracefully rather than refusing outright. Anthropic said they were tuned to fire in "less than 5% of sessions," meaning the overwhelming majority of the time you were talking to Fable itself.</Sidenote> This was never a model held back from the public. It was offered to the public — broadly, cheaply, with guardrails intact. What happened next is the story.

## the-mythos-sibling

Fable 5 and Mythos 5 are the same underlying model. The difference is the guardrails. Mythos 5 runs "with the safeguards lifted in some areas" and is deployed only to organizations that have "already been approved" for advanced access; Mythos had been "initially limited to a handful of partners due to cybersecurity concerns." Fable is that same capability with the safeguards left on, packaged for general use. So the family is really one model wearing two access policies: a guarded public face (Fable) and an unguarded, partner-only build (Mythos). That matters for what came next, because the safeguards that distinguish Fable from Mythos are exactly the ones the US government would come to say had been bypassed.

## the-recall

On June 12, 2026, at 5:21 p.m. ET, the US government — citing national-security authorities — issued an export-control directive to suspend all access to Fable 5 and Mythos 5 "by any foreign national, whether inside or outside the United States, including foreign national Anthropic employees." <Sidenote label="why a foreign-national rule disables it for everyone">Because Anthropic could not partition its global, multinational user base and workforce cleanly enough to keep foreign nationals out while serving everyone else, the only compliant move was to take both models down for all customers. Access to every other Anthropic model was unaffected.</Sidenote> The net effect was that Anthropic had to abruptly disable both models for *all* customers to comply. The government's stated concern was specific: it believed it had become aware of a method of "jailbreaking" Fable 5 — bypassing the safeguards meant to limit its use for cybersecurity tasks such as identifying software vulnerabilities. Anthropic disagreed publicly that a single narrow potential jailbreak should justify recalling a model deployed to hundreds of millions of people, warning that the same standard applied industry-wide "would essentially halt all new model deployments for all frontier model providers." As of late June, both models remain offline, with no confirmed restoration date. ## verdict

**Can you use it?** No. Fable 5 has been offline since June 12 under a legally binding export-control directive, with restoration neither confirmed nor dated. For a few days you could; now no one can.

**Should it have been pulled?** This is the genuinely hard part, and reasonable people split on it. The government's logic — a frontier model whose cyber safeguards can be bypassed is an export-controllable capability — is not absurd. Anthropic's rebuttal — that a narrow jailbreak finding shouldn't recall a product already in hundreds of millions of hands, and that the precedent would freeze frontier deployment industry-wide — is also not absurd. What's new here isn't a company gating its own model; it's a government reaching in to switch one off, which makes Fable 5 a precedent more than a product.

**The bottom line:** Fable 5 is less a model to evaluate than a turning point to watch — the moment frontier access stopped being purely a vendor's decision. But unlike a system that never left the lab, this one we actually got to use, and that brief opening, more than any spec, is what makes its closing matter.
